The appendices contain templates for covers for PPUC’s water and wastewater tariff petition. 2 The Revenue Requirement and Cost Allocation Any tariff methodology has three principal steps, shown in Figure 2.1. A summary of this process is that costs are first aggregated into a total; portions of this total are divided and assigned to each customer class; and then tariffs are designed to recover the portion of costs assigned to each customer class. These Guidelines specify how PPUC’s revenue requirement should be estimated and how costs should be allocated among PPUC’s customer classes.

Figure 2.1: Steps Required to Set Tariffs This section of the Guidelines provides direction for PPUC on the first and second steps in the water and wastewater tariff-setting process, namely, how to estimate the revenue requirements (Section 2.1) and how to develop a cost allocation study (Section 2.2).

It is important to note that these guidelines apply to costs associated with the delivery of water and wastewater service only. Revenues from electricity service must not be used to subsidize PPUC’s water and wastewater operations, and vice-versa, pursuant to Section 414 of the Utilities Consolidation Act. 2.1 Determining the Revenue Requirement PPUC’s revenue requirement is the total amount of revenue required to recover its costs of providing water and wastewater service, where such costs include prudent operating and maintenance (O&M) costs, capital expenditure (CAPEX) costs, and reasonable rates of returns on debt and equity required to finance the CAPEX.

The steps in determining the revenue requirement are as follows:

Select a test year Aggregate costs 2 Forecast costs and revenues 1.1 Selecting the Test Year The revenue requirement is determined by comparing the revenues of PPUC to its operating and capital costs during a “test year” to determine the adequacy of existing tariffs to recover costs. In submitting its tariff petition, PPUC should use an historic test year, adjusted for known and measurable changes. Such adjustments should be limited to:

Anomalies, in other words, the effects of unusual circumstances (e.g., an unusually dry year) Known Changes (anticipating the evolution of the business from the past to the future, such as changes in wage expenses resulting from a negotiated labor agreement or increases in costs of fuel or electricity) Adjustments or “true ups” from previous years (reconciling for previous differences between actual revenue and the revenue requirement).

The historic test year selected should be PPUC’s most recent fiscal year, adjusted for any of the above factors that are relevant. 2.1.2 Aggregating costs PPUC’s water and wastewater revenue requirement is calculated as the sum of the sum of its prudent and efficient costs of providing water and wastewater service, less other revenue and any adjustment for excess network losses. Operating and maintenance expenses (OP) O&M expenses refer to the ongoing costs of maintaining and operating water and wastewater utility equipment. This category consists of line items typically found on a company’s income statement. The O&M expenses included in the revenue requirement include all variable and fixed operating costs, but excluding interest payments on debt, and excluding any non-cash items such as provisions or depreciation.1 Debt service obligations (DS) Debt service obligations include principal and interest payments on PPUC’s long-term loans. By recovering the cost of principal payments, PPUC is able to able to repay lenders the cost of the investment; by recovering the cost of interest payments, PPUC is able to pay lenders the return on their investment.

Cash-paid capital improvements (CR) and depreciation on those assets (DCR) Cash-paid or “pay-as-you-go” capital improvements (CR) are capital expenditure projects that are paid for out of current year tariff revenues. Because such investments 1 Interest payments on debt are covered under the debt service (DS) component of the revenue requirement. are not debt financed, it is necessary to allow PPUC to recover a return of the investment through a depreciation charge (DCR) on the assets.

Some of PPUC’s cash-paid capital improvements are grant-funded. PEWA will not allow recovery of the cost of or any return on grant-funded investments.

Contribution to a Financial Stabilization Fund (FSS) Financial stabilization funds are a critically important component of the revenue requirement when a cash needs approach is needed. Capital financial stabilization funds should provide PPUC with enough revenue to cover a reasonable level of cash- paid capital expenditure on a year-to-year basis (for smaller projects that cannot be easily debt-financed or grant-funded). Financial stabilization funds should be used to cover working capital needs where credit lines are not available and may also include set-asides for anomalous events that cause unusual fluctuations in operating costs.

PEWA will consider allowing in the revenue requirement two types of financial stabilization funds:

Capital financial stabilization fund. A capital financial stabilization fund is used by utilities to fund larger capital projects. The establishment of a dedicated account allows the utility to self-fund projects absent the availability grant funds or commercial loans. The type of projects to be funded would typically be higher-cost capital projects that add assets with more than eight years of service life or extend the life of existing assets more than eight years. The size of the fund and the amount of annual contributions should be determined by PPUC’s capital plan and the prospects of obtaining other funding sources.

Operating financial stabilization fund. The revenues a utility receives do not always match the time when expenses are incurred. One approach to correct for this imbalance is to establish an operating financial stabilization fund to accommodate these routine fluctuations in revenues and expenses.

The size of the operating reserve is typically set as a percentage of the annual operating budget. A common approach is to use 45 days or 12.5 percent of the annual operating expense budget as the basis for the operating financial stabilization fund. PEWA will review proposals by PPUC for the appropriate number to be used. PEWA recognizes that PPUC may wish to propose an operating financial stabilization fund which provides it with sufficient liquidity to pay for the electricity provided by PPUC’s electric power operations (EPO).

Excess Losses (ELA) Losses are a natural part of transporting water. The cost of losses should be considered a normal operating expense, included in the revenue requirement.

However, losses outside of normal expectations are evidence of poor condition of infrastructure, poor metering/billing and/or poor management. PEWA may disallow the pass-through of some level of excess losses to customers. The adjustment for excess losses is equal to the units of lost water above the set threshold, times the average cost of extracting, collecting, treating and transporting water to the local network.

Other revenues (OR) Other revenues include revenue derived from sources other than tariffs for provision of utility services. These revenues may come from operational specific and miscellaneous services, such as connection fees, or from non-operating sources, such as interest income or rental income. Such other revenues are subtracted from the revenue requirement because the costs, if any, of providing such services should be recovered from those who benefit from the services, and not from water and wastewater customers.

Other revenues should not include revenues from provision of electricity services, which are decided under a separate process overseen by PEWA. 2.2 Developing a Cost Allocation Study PPUC shall, as part of its request to modify tariffs, prepare an Allocated Cost of Service Study (ACOSS) or update an existing ACOSS. The ACOSS must include estimates of the revenue requirement disaggregated by business segment and function (functionalization), the manner of cost causation (classification), and customer class (allocation). The cost-of-service study must not reflect any cross-subsidies or external subsidies.2 The steps required to allocate the revenue requirement are the following:

Assign the revenue requirement components by business segment Assign costs by causation Assign costs to customer classes Each of these steps is described below.

Assigning RR by business segment In reporting its costs of providing water and wastewater service by business segment and function, PPUC should categorize all costs as relating to extraction, collection, production, treatment, transmission, distribution, or administration. Other, additional categories may be introduced by PPUC as appropriate.

PPUC may propose methodologies for allocating shared operating expenses between business function and segments. Such methods may include allocations based on the proportion of direct labor assigned to each function or segment, or some other method that PPUC is able to justify.

Assigning costs by causation In assigning costs by manner of cost causation, PPUC will identify whether the costs are related to:

Commodity costs, which vary with the volume of water used or wastewater produced. Such costs typically include electricity, chemicals and other variable operating costs.

Demand costs, which vary with rate of customer demand on the system at a given point in time. Such may include fixed operating costs.

Customer costs, which are costs associated with serving customers, regardless of volume or rate of use. Such costs typically include the costs of metering, billing, and other administrative functions.

“preferential” means that the tariff may not recover PPUC’s full cost of serving that customer. 3 The Tariff Petition and Review Process This section describes the tariff petition and review process, from PPUC’s petition to the issuance of PEWA’s decision. The steps in the process are described in the subsections below: Section 3.1 describes the process for initiating a tariff revision, Section 3.2 describes the discovery period, Section 3.7 describes the public hearing, Section 3.8 describes the issuance of the tariff decision, and Section 3.9 describes steps after the decision has been issued. 3.1 Initiation of a Tariff Revision PEWA will consider proposals for new rates annually, but may consider more frequent adjustments if it determines that such adjustments are in the mutual interest of PPUC and customers or if PPUC can demonstrate that, without such adjustment, it would experience financial hardship. PPUC may be considered to be experiencing a financial hardship if revenues are insufficient to cover prudently incurred and efficient expenses, where such expenses include: a. Unexpected repair and maintenance expenses, as may result from a natural disaster b. Revenues to finance existing debt-service requirements c. Unexpectedly high costs associated with expense items beyond the managerial control of PPUC, such as electricity and chemicals purchase costs, inflation, or other extraordinary costs beyond PPUC’s control d. Other unexpected and unusual costs PPUC may incur, subject to PEWA approval.

A capital investment plan should include, at a minimum, the following components:

Review of prior year capital investments. The investment plan should begin with a summary of the investments made in the previous year. This summary shall address any differences between PPUC’s proposed and actual investments and provide explanation for any major changes.

Objectives and summary of the proposed plan. The investment plan should summarize PPUC’s capital investment strategy for the tariff year and any subsequent years covered by the plan (capital investment programs typically span three to five years). This plan needs to be tied to explicit operational objectives and goals. These objectives and goals can already be established as part of a business plan or another strategy agreed on with PEWA (e.g., a loss-reduction strategy or network expansion plan). If such objectives or goals have changed or new goals have been added PPUC needs to elaborate on these updates. The summary of the investment plan will then describe the investment activities to be undertaken over the duration of the plan and explain how these activities will contribute to the stated goals and objectives (including, critically, improvements foreseen in service quality).

Overview of projects and anticipated funding sources for three-year plan. The core of the investment plan should be a table listing each of the projects to be completed over the three-year period, a description of the project, the division of PPUC that will carry out the work, the funds to be disbursed in each year of the plan, and the source of the funds (donor grant, own funds/cash, donor loan, private loan, government grant, etc.) Historical and projected asset base. The investment plan should also include a summary of PPUC’s asset base for the most recent three-year period and the projected asset base that will result from the proposed investment strategy. The asset base should be disaggregated by item as established in PPUC’s chart of accounts. 3.4 Demand and Load Forecast Model PPUC should submit any model it has used to develop sales and peak demand projections shown in the Forms. The model should be provided in a Microsoft Excel file with all formulas and cell references intact.

Demand forecasts should always be accompanied by projections of the number of customers for each class for each of the forecast years. The customer projections should, in turn, be tied to network expansion and customer growth plans for the forecast period. In other words, the projections should use the current number of customers as a base and then increase class size by the number of connections anticipated for each year. Though not necessary, PPUC can also propose other adjustments to customer number projections based on any anticipated economic and financial changes. Should such changes be included the assumptions and any relevant supporting documentation should be clearly identified. 3.5 Other Information The PEWA may, as part of its tariff decisions, require a utility to submit specific additional information as part of its future tariff petitions; PPUC should ensure it complies with any such requirements. Purpose of Public Hearings The purpose of public hearings is to solicit the views of PPUC customers on proposed changes to tariffs, and the reasons for such changes. PPUC will make arrangements to host and conduct public hearings, in coordination with and participation of PEWA, respective state authorities, consumer groups (represented, for example, by non- governmental organizations or industry associations), and individual consumers.4 Participation in Public Hearings Persons making representations may make written representations to be submitted by the date fixed by PPUC for such submissions; or may make oral representations on the date fixed for the public hearing. During the public hearings, upon presenting the proposed changes in the tariff, reasons for the changes, members of the public shall be given opportunity to raise questions, concerns and suggestions for the PPUC and relevant state authorities to respond, record and react.

Public Hearings are open for all who wish to attend as observers. Persons wishing to make representations must do so by registering with PEWA as presenters no less than two (5) business days before the scheduled hearing. PEWA will maintain a list of registered presenters, and develop an agenda/timetable for their presentations. The agenda and timetable will be posted to PEWA’s and PPUC’s websites.

(2) A short statement that sets forth the reasons for the rate increase

(3) The time and place of the public hearing

2. If PPUC has filled in the relevant Forms, analyze the contents of the Forms on their own merits (i.e., without reviewing any other sources of information)

3. Develop discovery questions on this basis

5. If PPUC has filled in the relevant Forms, compare PPUC’s submission (in the 6 Forms) to the other sources of data PEWA has available Develop discovery questions on this basis

7. If PPUC has not filled in the relevant Forms, analyze what other information 8 PEWA has available Develop discovery questions on this basis

9. Send discovery questions to PPUC.

The following subsections have more detailed steps to follow for each component of the revenue requirement. 5.1 Operating and Maintenance Expenses PEWA will review the prudence and reasonableness of PPUC’s water and wastewater- related O&M expenses. To review the O&M expenses component of the revenue requirement, PEWA does the following:

PEWA reviews tariff petition Form W6.

PEWA undertakes the following analyses: – PEWA analyzes growth rates from year to year:

Are growth rates suspiciously consistent? Are there unusual fluctuations in historic data? If so, PEWA will ask about those, especially if they are “big ticket” items in relation to total costs. – PEWA assesses whether the proposed value for the tariff year (after the adjustment) is considerably higher or lower than in previous years. If so, PEWA will ask why. – PEWA assesses whether PPUC gave its accounts on a cash basis or an accruals basis. PPUC should provide them on a cash basis. – PEWA identifies whether there are any non-cash expenses included (e.g., depreciation, provisions for certain expenses, etc.). – PEWA looks for any unusual expenses that are not expected to be seen in an income statement. If there are any, PEWA will ask about them.

If Form W6 is not filled, PEWA will identify other possible sources of information it has on file that could help answer questions. Income statements (also called profit & loss statements) are the single best source of such information. Cashflow statements can also be helpful in distinguishing cash expenses from non-cash expenses (especially if PPUC only provided income statements on an accruals basis).

PEWA will review the prudence and reasonableness of PPUC’s expenditures using a variety of techniques: – Comparing the proposed expenditures to historic expenditures by PPUC – Consulting international and regional benchmarks for good practice, using sources such as the Pacific Water and Wastewater Association. – Comparing historic expenditures to what PPUC had budgeted for that historic year – Requesting additional documentation and explanation from PPUC. 5.2 Debt Service To review the debt-service component of the revenue requirement, PEWA does the following:

PEWA reviews tariff petition Forms W3 through W5 (Form W5 is the most important). If Form W5 is filled, PEWA makes certain that the principal amount, as indicated in Form W5, matches the totals for debt to be serviced in Forms W3 and W4.

PEWA undertakes the following analyses: – PEWA assesses whether any interest rates, loan tenors, or principal amounts look unusually low or unusually high. If they do, PEWA submits discovery questions about them, asking for additional documentation. – If loans are from multilateral lending banks (e.g., Asian Development Bank or World Bank), PEWA checks to see whether there are grace periods (there typically are) and what the tenors of the loans are (they should be quite long). – If loans are from commercial banks, PEWA checks to see whether they are within the range of what might be expected for commercial lending in Palau. – If anything looks questionable, PEWA will ask for supporting documentation (e.g., loan agreements) – PEWA assesses whether the debt-financed CAPEX indicated in the Forms is consistent with PPUC’s investment plans. PEWA will ask for copies of those investment plans, if it does not have them already.

If Form W5 is not filled, the PEWA will identify other possible sources of information it has on file that could help to answer questions.

If PEWA does not have other sources on file, it will ask for other sources (e.g., loan agreements). The PEWA will then use these documents to develop questions. 5.3 Cash-Paid Capital Expenditure and Depreciation on Cash-Paid Capital To review the cash-paid capital expenditure and depreciation on cash-paid capital components of the revenue requirement, PEWA does the following:

PEWA reviews tariff petitions Forms W3 and W4 and ensures that principals on any debt-financed CAPEX is accurately reflected in Form W5.

PEWA undertakes the following analyses: – PEWA assesses whether the cash-paid CAPEX indicated in the Forms is consistent with PPUC’s investment plans. The PEWA will ask for copies of those investment plans, if it does not have them already. – PEWA assesses whether PPUC is claiming “cash-paid” CAPEX for assets that are grant-funded. – PEWA considers the timing of the CAPEX PPUC has indicated for the coming year:

Will these be “used and usable”? If they are not “used and usable,” is there some justification for including their costs in tariffs (e.g., they are big investments that would otherwise put the company at hardship if the costs were not recovered through tariffs)? – PEWA asks what depreciation rates PPUC assumes for these assets. – PEWA consider whether these depreciation rates are justified given the expected life of the asset.

If Forms W3 and W4 are not filled, PEWA will identify other possible sources of information it has on file that could help to answer questions (e.g., business plans or investment plans).

If PEWA does not have other sources on file, it will ask PPUC for them.

If PPUC does not provide sufficient justification, PEWA is under no obligation to include their estimated CAPEX in the revenue requirement.

The capital investment plan (a requirement of the tariff petition under Section 3.3 of these Guidelines) provides PEWA with important information on the historical and future investment plans of PPUC. As part of the tariff review, PEWA staff will undertake the following tasks to assess the prudence of capital investment plan:

Compare prior year plans to targets. The ability to set and meet investment goals is important for the physical and financial integrity of utility operations. Utilities that consistently miss investment targets may be operating ineffectively and may require change in management. PEWA compares both projected and actual amounts spent on investments as well as the proposed projects to the actual projects carried out.

Evaluate fit between objectives and investment plan. It is important that investments are tied to specific operational objectives and goals. With limited resources, and numerous options for investments, part of PEWA’s role in evaluating an investment plan is to determine whether the proposal represents the best use of the funds. PPUC’s own operational goals and other national policy goals represent the priorities that have been established for PPUC. PEWA determines whether each proposed project fits within the scope of PPUC’s priorities. If a project is not intended to contribute to a specific objective or goal PEWA will request additional information on why the project is included and determine whether the allocated funds could be used for another priority purpose. PEWA can provide direction to PPUC on what projects should or should not be pursued in the final tariff decision.

Assess potential tariff impact of investment plan. Capital investments can have profound impacts on the tariff levels needed to achieve cost recovery. PEWA assesses what the bill impact will be if the plan is implemented as proposed. If an investment plan would result in a substantial jump in tariffs in one year this may signal a need to delay or spread out some of the investments over future years. 5.4 Contribution to a financial stabilization fund To review the financial stabilization fund component of the revenue requirement, PEWA does the following:

PEWA undertakes the following analyses: – PEWA determines whether there is any law which requires the utilities to set up a financial stabilization fund. – If PPUC has proposed such a fund, PEWA determines what type of fund PPUC has proposed. The Tariff Guidelines allow for operating financial stabilization accounts and capital financial stabilization accounts.

PEWA asks on what basis PPUC has proposed the financial stabilization account (i.e., how PPUC made its estimates). If PPUC is proposing a financial stabilization account for CAPEX, it should ideally be based on an investment plan.

If PPUC is proposing some other type of financial stabilization account (e.g., operating reserve), PEWA ensures that PPUC justifies how it has arrived at the amount budgeted for that account.

If PPUC has not proposed any such account, or have not supported its proposal with evidence, the PEWA is not obliged to allow anything, but may want to remind PPUC if it has a legal obligation to establish such an account. 5.5 Allowed Losses To review the allowed losses component of the revenue requirement, the PEWA does the following:

PEWA will review tariff petition Form W7.

PEWA undertakes the following analyses: – PEWA determines whether the historic data are suspiciously consistent. – PEWA looks for unusual fluctuations in historic data. If there are any, the PEWA will ask about those. – PEWA assesses whether the loss percentages correspond with other numbers on production and sales reported elsewhere. Alternatives include business plans, annual reports, press reports, or technical reports. – PEWA will ask how PPUC measures losses. 5.6 Other Revenue To review the other revenue component of the revenue requirement, PEWA does the following:

PEWA will identify possible sources of information it has on file that could help answer questions. Income statements (profit & loss statements) are the best source, especially if accompanied by notes (e.g., in an annual report or audit report).

PEWA undertakes the following analyses: – PEWA will ask for documentation on each potential source of other revenue. – PEWA will ensure that, if one business segment is selling to another, there is “arms-length pricing” (i.e., that one business segment is selling to the other at the same rates they would sell to the market).
